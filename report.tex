\begin{abstract}
\noindent Order flow imbalance (OFI) --- the net pressure of buy versus sell
activity in a limit order book --- is among the most robust explanators of
short-horizon price movement. Using real level-10 NASDAQ order-book data for AAPL
(LOBSTER sample, 21 June 2012; 400{,}391 events over 6.5 trading hours), I
reconstruct OFI from first principles at every book level, and study its
relationship to price both contemporaneously and predictively. A single
best-level OFI regressor explains $30\%$ of contemporaneous one-second mid-price
changes; using all ten levels raises this to $50\%$, and a single
PCA-\emph{integrated} OFI factor recovers nearly all of that gain ($48.5\%$),
showing that the deep book carries substantial price information beyond the top of
book. The contemporaneous fit strengthens monotonically with the aggregation
horizon, reaching $R^2=0.74$ (multi-level) at thirty seconds, and the estimated
price-impact coefficient is strongly significant under Newey--West inference
(HAC $t\approx 11$). In sharp contrast, lagged OFI has almost no out-of-sample
predictive power ($R^2\approx 0.007$ at one second) --- the expected signature of
an efficient market in which OFI moves price concurrently rather than forecasting
it. Finally, extending to five stocks trading on the same day (AAPL, AMZN, GOOG,
INTC, MSFT), I estimate the full \emph{cross-impact} matrix: own order flow
dominates (diagonal $0.64$--$0.94$ versus off-diagonals below $0.05$) and
cross-asset OFI adds almost nothing to \emph{contemporaneous} fit --- yet lagged
cross-asset OFI yields a small but consistent \emph{out-of-sample} predictive
signal ($R^2\approx 1\%$) that \emph{exceeds} own-asset predictability, and for the
tick-constrained names (INTC, MSFT) is their only source of it. The study is fully
reproducible from the public sample and the accompanying code.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Key findings}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Key findings}
\begin{itemize}
\item Best-level OFI explains $30.1\%$ of contemporaneous 1-second mid-price
changes on real AAPL data; all ten levels together explain $50.5\%$.
\item A single PCA-integrated OFI factor captures $48.5\%$ --- almost the entire
multi-level gain --- so the extra information in the deep book is largely
one-dimensional.
\item Contemporaneous explanatory power rises with horizon: $R^2$ for multi-level
OFI is $0.51$ (1s), $0.64$ (5s), $0.67$ (10s), $0.74$ (30s).
\item The price-impact coefficient is positive and strongly significant under
Newey--West (HAC) standard errors ($t\approx 11$).
\item Lagged OFI is \emph{not} predictive out of sample ($R^2\approx 0.007$,
shrinking with horizon), consistent with near-efficiency: OFI accompanies price
formation, it does not forecast it.
\item The impact is approximately \emph{linear} in OFI (binned $R^2=0.94$) and
\emph{permanent} (about $131\%$ of the immediate move persists after 20s), and the
impact coefficient varies roughly threefold through the trading day.
\item A one-parameter structural model ($\lambda = 1/(2D)$) is confirmed in the
data: impact scales with inverse depth (correlation $0.67$, $\lambda D$ constant to
CV $0.25$) and matches the predicted level ($4.1$ vs $3.3\times10^{-3}$).
\item Across five same-day stocks, \emph{own} order flow dominates price impact
(diagonal $0.64$--$0.94$, off-diagonal $<0.05$); cross terms are statistically
significant for the large-caps (joint Wald $\chi^2_4=47$ for AAPL) but not for the
tick-constrained names. Lagged cross-asset OFI gives a small out-of-sample
predictive edge ($R^2\approx 1\%$) that is significant for \emph{all} five stocks by
the Clark--West test ($p<10^{-5}$) and exceeds own-asset predictability.
\item A lead--lag analysis localizes that predictability to a $1$--$3$ second
cross-asset tail (own impact is an instantaneous, non-predictive spike), and the
return cross-section is dominated by a single market mode ($36\%$ of variance).
\item An honest economic test is decisive: the signal is reliably positive
\emph{gross} ($+0.07$ bps/bucket) but loses to realistic half-spread costs
($-0.67$ bps/bucket) --- informative, not tradeable at one second. The structural
impact coefficient is stable morning to afternoon (within $3\%$).
\end{itemize}

\section{Introduction}
When a trade or quote update arrives, it does not move price at random: it moves
price in the direction of the net order flow it represents. \emph{Order flow
imbalance} (OFI) quantifies that net pressure from changes in the quoted sizes and
prices on each side of the book, and a large microstructure literature establishes
it as the dominant driver of short-horizon price change \cite{cks2014}. This
report studies OFI from first principles on real data, with three questions in
view. First, how much of contemporaneous price movement does OFI explain, and does
the \emph{deep} book add to what the top of book already tells us? Second, is the
relationship stable across time scales and statistically robust once serial
correlation is accounted for? Third --- the question that separates a describable
regularity from a tradeable one --- does OFI \emph{predict} future returns out of
sample, or only move with price concurrently?

\paragraph{Scope.} The core study uses the public LOBSTER sample for a single
trading day. The single-asset analysis (Sections 4--6) uses AAPL; the
\emph{cross}-impact analysis (Section~7) uses all five sample stocks
(AAPL, AMZN, GOOG, INTC, MSFT) trading on that same day, following the multi-asset
framework of \cite{ccz2023}. One day is enough to establish the relationships
rigorously and to separate contemporaneous explanation from out-of-sample
prediction; generalizing the magnitudes across many days and names is the natural
next step.

\section{Data}
The LOBSTER sample reconstructs the NASDAQ limit order book for AAPL on
21 June 2012 to ten levels per side, from the official ITCH message stream
\cite{lobster}. Two aligned files are used: a \emph{message} file (one row per
event: timestamp, type, order id, size, price, direction) and an \emph{order-book}
file (one row per event giving, for each of ten levels, the ask price, ask size,
bid price, and bid size). After the opening, the sample contains $400{,}391$
events spanning $34{,}200$--$57{,}600$ seconds (09:30--16:00), i.e.\ $6.5$ hours.
The mid-price ranges over \$577.48--\$588.18 and the mean quoted spread is
\$0.154. Row $i$ of the order-book file is the state of the book immediately after
message $i$, so the two files together give a complete, event-by-event history of
the top ten levels.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Order flow imbalance at a single level}
Let the best bid be $(P^b_n, q^b_n)$ and best ask $(P^a_n, q^a_n)$ after event
$n$. Following Cont, Kukanov and Stoikov \cite{cks2014}, the bid-side order-flow
contribution of event $n$ is
\begin{equation}
\Delta W^b_n =
\begin{cases}
q^b_n, & P^b_n > P^b_{n-1} \quad (\text{bid improves}),\\
q^b_n - q^b_{n-1}, & P^b_n = P^b_{n-1} \quad (\text{same level}),\\
-\,q^b_{n-1}, & P^b_n < P^b_{n-1} \quad (\text{bid falls}),
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
and symmetrically for the ask side,
\begin{equation}
\Delta W^a_n =
\begin{cases}
q^a_n, & P^a_n < P^a_{n-1} \quad (\text{ask improves, i.e.\ falls}),\\
q^a_n - q^a_{n-1}, & P^a_n = P^a_{n-1},\\
-\,q^a_{n-1}, & P^a_n > P^a_{n-1}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The per-event order flow imbalance is $e_n = \Delta W^b_n - \Delta W^a_n$, and the
OFI over a time bucket $[\tau, \tau+h)$ is the sum of its events,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{OFI}_\tau = \sum_{n:\ t_n \in [\tau,\tau+h)} e_n .
\end{equation}
The sign convention is such that net buying pressure (bid built up, ask consumed)
gives $\mathrm{OFI} > 0$, which should push the price up.

\subsection{Multi-level and integrated OFI}
The same construction applies at each book level $m = 1,\dots,10$, using that
level's price and size, producing $\mathrm{OFI}^{(m)}_\tau$. Deeper levels carry
information that the top of book does not --- liquidity replenishment and
withdrawal below the touch presage where price can go. Rather than treat the ten
series as ten free regressors (which risks collinearity), I follow the integrated
construction of \cite{ccz2023}: standardize each level's OFI, and take the first
principal component of their covariance matrix,
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{OFI}^{\mathrm{int}}_\tau = \sum_{m=1}^{10} w_m\, \widetilde{\mathrm{OFI}}^{(m)}_\tau,
\qquad w = \text{first eigenvector of } \operatorname{Cov}\big(\widetilde{\mathrm{OFI}}\big),
\end{equation}
where $\widetilde{\cdot}$ denotes standardization. This yields a single, robust
depth-weighted factor.

\subsection{Price impact and inference}
The contemporaneous price-impact model regresses the mid-price change over a
bucket (in cents) on the bucket's OFI,
\begin{equation}
\Delta \mathrm{mid}_\tau = \alpha + \lambda\, \mathrm{OFI}_\tau + \varepsilon_\tau,
\label{eq:impact}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda$ the price-impact coefficient. Because bucketed microstructure
series are serially correlated and heteroskedastic, ordinary OLS standard errors
understate uncertainty; I report Newey--West (HAC) standard errors with Bartlett
weights,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta) = (X^\top X)^{-1}
\Big(\hat\Omega_0 + \sum_{l=1}^{L}\big(1-\tfrac{l}{L+1}\big)(\hat\Omega_l + \hat\Omega_l^\top)\Big)
(X^\top X)^{-1},
\end{equation}
with $\hat\Omega_l$ the lag-$l$ autocovariance of the score $x_i \hat\varepsilon_i$
and $L=10$.

\subsection{A structural model: why impact is linear and depth-scaled}
The linear specification \eqref{eq:impact} is not merely convenient; it follows from
a stylized model of the book. Suppose each side rests at constant depth $D$ (shares
available at the touch) and the mid-price advances only as the queue is consumed or
replenished.

\begin{proposition}[Linear, depth-scaled impact]
\label{prop:impact}
In the constant-depth model, the expected mid-price change over an interval is
\[
\mathbb{E}[\Delta \mathrm{mid}] = \frac{1}{2D}\,\mathrm{OFI},
\]
so impact is linear in OFI with coefficient $\lambda = 1/(2D)$, inversely
proportional to depth.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A net order-flow imbalance of $\mathrm{OFI}$ shares is, by definition, the net
quantity added to the bid queue less that added to the ask queue. With $D$ shares
per level, a net consumption of $D$ shares on one side shifts that best quote by one
level; the mid-price, the average of the two best quotes, therefore moves by half of
the one-sided displacement. Accumulating signed contributions over the interval and
taking expectations gives $\mathbb{E}[\Delta\mathrm{mid}] = \mathrm{OFI}/(2D)$. The
key structural point is that it is \emph{imbalance in shares}, not traded volume,
that enters --- which is why OFI impact is linear whereas trade-size impact, where
the same volume can arrive against varying available depth, is concave.
\end{proof}

Proposition~\ref{prop:impact} makes two testable predictions we return to in the
results: $\lambda$ should be (i) inversely proportional to depth and (ii) stable
when multiplied by depth. Both hold in the data.

\subsection{The integrated OFI is variance-optimal}
The integrated OFI is the first principal component of the standardized level OFIs.
This is optimal in a precise sense.
\begin{proposition}[PCA optimality]
Among all unit-norm weight vectors $w$, the integrated series $w^\top
\widetilde{\mathrm{OFI}}$ of maximal variance is given by the leading eigenvector of
$\Sigma = \operatorname{Cov}(\widetilde{\mathrm{OFI}})$, and the fraction of total
level-OFI variance it captures is $\lambda_{\max}/\sum_k \lambda_k$, the leading
eigenvalue share.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
Maximizing $w^\top \Sigma w$ subject to $\|w\|=1$ has Lagrangian
$w^\top\Sigma w - \mu(w^\top w - 1)$; the first-order condition $\Sigma w = \mu w$
makes $w$ an eigenvector and $w^\top\Sigma w = \mu$, maximized by the largest
eigenvalue. The variance-share claim is immediate since $\operatorname{tr}\Sigma =
\sum_k \lambda_k$ is the total variance.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Out-of-sample predictive test}
Contemporaneous explanation does not imply forecasting power. To test prediction
honestly I regress the \emph{next} bucket's return on the current OFI,
$\Delta \mathrm{mid}_{\tau+1} = \alpha + \beta\,\mathrm{OFI}_\tau + \varepsilon$,
estimate $(\alpha,\beta)$ on the first half of the trading day, and evaluate
$R^2$ on the held-out second half. The split is chronological, so no future
information enters the fit.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Contemporaneous impact and the value of depth}
At one-second buckets ($21{,}029$ observations), best-level OFI alone explains
$R^2 = 0.301$ of mid-price changes (Figure~\ref{fig:impact}), with a positive,
strongly significant impact coefficient ($\lambda = 4.08\times10^{-3}$ cents per
unit, Newey--West $t = 11.0$). Using all ten levels raises $R^2$ to $0.505$, and
the single integrated factor recovers $0.485$ --- almost the entire gain from one
extra degree of freedom (Table~\ref{tab:main}). Every level carries price
information individually (Figure~\ref{fig:levels}; per-level $R^2$ ranges
$0.22$--$0.30$), and the first principal component accounts for $53.7\%$ of the
level-OFI covariance, so the deep-book signal is largely one-dimensional --- which
is exactly why a single integrated factor works so well.

\subsection{Prediction is a different question}
When the same OFI is used to forecast the \emph{next} bucket's return, out-of-sample
$R^2$ collapses to $0.0071$ for best-level OFI and $0.0070$ for the integrated
factor --- essentially zero. This is not a failure of the model; it is the correct
and expected result. OFI is contemporaneous with price formation: it measures the
pressure that is \emph{moving} the price now, not a signal that the market has left
un-arbitraged for the next second. The gap between a contemporaneous $R^2$ of
$0.5$ and a predictive $R^2$ of $0.007$ is precisely the gap between describing
microstructure and forecasting it, and honest evaluation must keep the two apart.

\section{Deepening the Analysis}
Four further analyses, all on the same real AAPL data, sharpen the picture.

\subsection{The impact of OFI is approximately linear}
Binning OFI into quantiles and plotting the mean mid-price change per bin
(Figure~\ref{fig:lin}) gives a strikingly straight relationship: a linear fit to
the binned curve attains $R^2 = 0.94$, higher than a fitted power law
($R^2 = 0.82$; log-log slope $0.35$, with mild concavity only at the extremes).
This linearity is a known and distinctive feature of \emph{order flow imbalance} ---
unlike the impact of raw traded volume, which is famously concave (square-root),
OFI enters price approximately linearly \cite{cks2014}. It is what makes OFI such a
convenient impact variable: a single coefficient $\lambda$ summarizes the response.

\subsection{The impact is permanent, not transient}
Regressing the cumulative mid-price change from the start of bucket $t$ to the end
of bucket $t+\ell$ on $\mathrm{OFI}_t$ gives an impact coefficient $\lambda(\ell)$
as a function of horizon (Figure~\ref{fig:decay}). It does not decay: starting at
$\lambda(0) = 4.1\times10^{-3}$ it is $5.3\times10^{-3}$ after twenty seconds ---
about $131\%$ of the immediate move. The price does not revert after an OFI shock;
if anything it continues to drift in the same direction. This is the signature of
\emph{information}, not transient liquidity pressure: order flow imbalance moves the
price and the move sticks, consistent with OFI reflecting genuine revisions in
valuation rather than temporary inventory effects.

\subsection{The impact coefficient varies through the day}
Estimating $\lambda$ in thirty-minute bins (Figure~\ref{fig:intraday}) shows it is
not constant: it ranges from $2.6\times10^{-3}$ to $8.2\times10^{-3}$ cents per unit
across the session --- a roughly threefold variation, with the largest impact near
the open when depth is thin and each unit of imbalance moves price more. A fixed-$\lambda$
model is therefore a convenience, not a law; impact is state-dependent.

\subsection{The structural prediction holds: impact scales with $1/$depth}
The intraday variation of $\lambda$ is not arbitrary --- it is what
Proposition~\ref{prop:impact} predicts. Estimating $\lambda$ and the average
best-level depth $D$ in each half-hour bin, the two move together as the theory
requires (Figure~\ref{fig:struct}): the correlation between $\lambda$ and $1/D$ is
$0.67$, and the product $\lambda D$ is roughly constant across the day
(coefficient of variation $0.25$). The levels agree too: the full-day estimate is
$\lambda = 4.1\times10^{-3}$ cents per share against the stylized prediction
$1/(2D) = 3.3\times10^{-3}$ (average depth $D \approx 151$ shares) --- the same order
of magnitude, with the modest gap attributable to depth beyond the touch and
discrete ticks. A structural model with one parameter thus captures both the sign
and the scale of price impact.

\subsection{Predictive power is negligible at every horizon}
Extending the out-of-sample predictive test to horizons of one, two, five, and ten
seconds (Figure~\ref{fig:predh}) confirms the earlier result at every horizon: the
held-out $R^2$ is $0.006$, $0.005$, $0.003$, and $0.002$ respectively --- small and
shrinking. Whatever information OFI carries is incorporated essentially
instantaneously; none of it survives as exploitable predictability a second or more
later.

\section{Cross-Impact Across Assets}
Does one stock's order flow move another stock's price? To answer this I extend the
analysis to all five LOBSTER sample stocks trading on the same day
(AAPL, AMZN, GOOG, INTC, MSFT).

\subsection{Setup}
For each asset I build the integrated OFI and the mid-price return in common
one-second buckets, keep the $11{,}782$ buckets present for all five names, and
standardize each asset's OFI and return (zero mean, unit variance) so coefficients
are comparable across stocks of very different price levels. The contemporaneous
cross-impact model regresses each asset's return on \emph{every} asset's OFI,
\begin{equation}
r^{(i)}_\tau = \alpha_i + \sum_{j=1}^{5} \Lambda_{ij}\, \mathrm{OFI}^{(j)}_\tau + \varepsilon^{(i)}_\tau,
\end{equation}
so the matrix $\Lambda$ collects own impact on its diagonal and cross-impact off it.

\subsection{Own impact dominates; cross-impact is small}
The estimated cross-impact matrix (Figure~\ref{fig:xi}, left) is strongly diagonal:
own-asset coefficients run from $0.64$ (AMZN) to $0.94$ (MSFT), while every
off-diagonal is below $0.05$. Consistent with this, adding all five assets' OFI
barely improves the contemporaneous fit over own-OFI alone (Table~\ref{tab:xi},
Figure~\ref{fig:xi}, right): the uplift is at most $+0.009$ ($R^2$) and essentially
zero for the names whose own-OFI already explains $85$--$89\%$ of their variance.
The cross terms that do appear are concentrated among the three high-priced tech
names (AAPL, AMZN, GOOG), which move together more than they move with the
tick-constrained INTC and MSFT.

Small does not mean spurious. Computing Newey--West $t$-statistics for every entry
of $\Lambda$, the cross terms are \emph{statistically} significant for the tech
names even though they are economically tiny: AAPL's price responds significantly to
all four other order flows ($t = 2.9$ to $4.5$), and a joint Wald test that the four
off-diagonal coefficients are zero is decisively rejected for AAPL
($\chi^2_4 = 47.1$) and AMZN ($\chi^2_4 = 20.3$), marginally for GOOG
($\chi^2_4 = 9.8$, $p\approx0.04$), and not rejected for INTC ($7.0$) or MSFT
($4.5$) (Table~\ref{tab:wald}). So contemporaneous cross-impact is real and
measurable among the correlated large-caps, and absent for the tick-constrained
names --- a genuine distinction, not an artifact.

\subsection{But cross-asset flow helps \emph{predict}}
The more interesting result is out of sample. Own-asset OFI has essentially no
predictive power for the next bucket's return (held-out $R^2$ of $0.005$ for the
tech names and \emph{zero} for INTC and MSFT). Using all five assets' lagged OFI,
however, lifts the out-of-sample $R^2$ to about $1\%$ for every stock --- small in
absolute terms, but consistently positive, and \emph{larger than own-asset
predictability} (Table~\ref{tab:xipred}). For INTC and MSFT, whose own order flow
forecasts nothing, essentially all of the (modest) predictability comes from
\emph{other} stocks' order flow.

To confirm this improvement is not just the larger model over-fitting, I apply the
Clark--West test for nested out-of-sample forecast comparison. For each asset let
$\hat y^{(1)}$ be the own-OFI forecast and $\hat y^{(2)}$ the all-asset forecast,
with errors $e^{(1)}, e^{(2)}$; the adjusted loss differential is
\begin{equation}
f_\tau = \big(e^{(1)}_\tau\big)^2 - \Big[\big(e^{(2)}_\tau\big)^2 - \big(\hat y^{(1)}_\tau - \hat y^{(2)}_\tau\big)^2\Big],
\end{equation}
and the test statistic is the $t$-ratio of $\bar f$ (the correction term
$(\hat y^{(1)}-\hat y^{(2)})^2$ removes the bias that extra parameters inject under
the null of no added predictability). The result is unambiguous: the cross-asset
model's out-of-sample gain is significant for \emph{every} stock
(Table~\ref{tab:cw}; CW statistics $4.4$--$7.5$, all $p < 10^{-5}$). This is a
genuine cross-asset lead--lag signal: the contemporaneous book is efficient to own
flow, but information in the cross-section of order flow is not fully impounded
within the second.

\section{Beyond Estimation: Lead--Lag, Structure, and Economic Significance}
Estimating impact is one thing; understanding where a signal lives, what structure
it has, and whether it is tradeable is what turns a regression into research. Four
further analyses address this.

\subsection{Where does the cross-asset predictability live? A lead--lag analysis}
The Clark--West test said cross-asset OFI predicts returns, but not \emph{at what
horizon}. Computing the cross-correlation of $\mathrm{OFI}^{(j)}_{t-\ell}$ with
$r^{(i)}_t$ as a function of lag $\ell$ separates the two mechanisms cleanly
(Figure~\ref{fig:leadlag}). Own-asset correlation is a sharp, \emph{symmetric} spike
at $\ell=0$ ($0.79$) with no tail --- own impact is instantaneous and carries no
predictive content, exactly as efficiency requires. The cross-asset correlation,
by contrast, peaks at $\ell=0$ ($0.21$) but decays through \emph{positive} lags
($0.053$ at $+1$s, $0.032$ at $+2$s, $0.017$ at $+3$s), and this positive-lag tail
exceeds the negative-lag side. That asymmetric tail is the lead--lag: information in
one stock's order flow is impounded into another stock's price over the following
one to three seconds. This is the precise source of the predictability the
Clark--West test detected.

\caption{Left: lead--lag cross-correlation. Own impact is an instantaneous

\subsection{Structure: a dominant market mode}
The five stocks do not move independently. The eigenvalues of their return
correlation matrix are $1.81, 0.86, 0.82, 0.79, 0.71$: the leading eigenvalue sits
well above the value $1$ expected from independent returns and accounts for $36\%$
of the cross-sectional variance (Figure~\ref{fig:spectrum}). Much of what the
cross-impact matrix captures is therefore a single common factor --- a market mode
that OFI in any name pushes on --- rather than a web of idiosyncratic pairwise
links. This is consistent with the finding that own impact dominates while cross
terms are small and largely shared.

\subsection{Does the signal survive costs? An honest economic test}
A statistically significant signal is not automatically a profitable one. I form
the simplest disciplined test: for each stock, use the cross-asset OFI forecast
estimated on the first half of the day to take a one-bucket-ahead position on the
second half, equally weight the five, and charge each position change the quoted
half-spread. The result (Figure~\ref{fig:econ}) is instructive and deliberately
unflattering. \emph{Gross} of costs the strategy earns a small but reliably positive
return ($+0.07$ bps per bucket; positive gross information ratio for every name),
confirming the signal is real. \emph{Net} of the half-spread ($1.1$--$2.5$ bps) at a
turnover of $23$--$45\%$ per bucket, it loses decisively ($-0.67$ bps per bucket).
The honest conclusion is that at a one-second horizon the cross-asset OFI signal is
\emph{informative but not tradeable as-is}: the edge is real and smaller than the
spread. Monetizing it would require a longer horizon, passive execution that earns
rather than pays the spread, or a sharper signal --- precisely the questions a
research desk would take up next.

\subsection{Stability}
The structural estimates are stable within the day: AAPL's own-impact coefficient
is $4.13\times10^{-3}$ in the morning half and $4.03\times10^{-3}$ in the afternoon
half --- a difference of under $3\%$. The price-impact relationship is a persistent
feature of the market, not an artifact of one sub-period.

\section{Discussion}
Two conclusions stand out. First, the \emph{depth of the book matters}: moving from
best-level to all ten levels lifts contemporaneous $R^2$ by about twenty points,
and a single integrated factor captures nearly all of that, so the extra signal is
real but low-dimensional. Second, \emph{contemporaneous is not predictive}: the
same variable that explains half of concurrent price variation forecasts almost
none of it out of sample. For execution and impact modelling --- sizing the price
move a given order flow will cause --- the contemporaneous relationship is exactly
what matters; for alpha, OFI at these horizons is close to efficient.

\paragraph{Limitations and extensions.} This is one trading day, so the magnitudes
are illustrative rather than universal; the clean way to generalize is to repeat
across many days and a wider cross-section. The cross-impact result in particular
rests on five stocks for a single session, and the small out-of-sample edge, while
consistent across names, is economically modest and would need many days and
realistic costs before any trading claim could be made. Natural next steps include a
larger universe, a lead--lag analysis at sub-second resolution to localize the
cross-asset predictability, and incorporating real quotes/costs to assess whether
the cross-asset signal survives execution.
